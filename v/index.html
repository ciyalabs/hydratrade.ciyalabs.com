HydraTrade V: Paid to Wait

Funding spreads between exchanges, why they cannot be traded at size, and the older trade that can

Loghman SamaniCiya LabsNovember 2026

The same perpetual contract can charge longs 40 percent a year on one exchange and 5 percent on another. A trade that is short where funding is high and long where it is low collects the difference, while the two price moves cancel.

This report measures that difference between three large exchanges, called here the first, second and third, over five years, with every variant written down before it ran. The spread is real and wide, and it is wide exactly where almost nobody trades. Coins that change hands for less than 250 thousand dollars a day on the first exchange produced 92 percent of the profit of the best version, and once a modest liquidity floor is imposed the trade loses money. What survives is older and simpler. Holding bitcoin and ether while shorting their perpetuals earned 6.9 percent a year after the first exchange's fees in 2024 to 2026, with a deepest drawdown under one percent. It earns what the market's appetite for leverage pays, which was 34 percent in 2021 and about 2 percent so far in 2026.

Part one

Why the fast version is closed, and the slow one is not

Buying a coin on one exchange and selling it on another when their prices differ is the first trade most people think of, and it is one of the most thoroughly competed in all of finance. A first attempt at it in this project stopped at the speed of a public interface, which is exactly where the literature says it should stop.

When the same asset trades in two places, the gap between the prices is usually a few basis points and lives for milliseconds. Budish, Cramton and Shim show for equity futures that such gaps are taken by whoever is fastest, so competition becomes a race for speed rather than a contest of ideas.1 Foucault, Kozhan and Tham find the same in currency markets, where most triangular arbitrage opportunities vanish within a second.2 Crypto had large and lasting gaps in 2017 and 2018, but Makarov and Schoar trace the large ones to capital controls between countries,3 and within open markets they shrank sharply after 2018.4

Funding is different in kind. A perpetual future has no expiry, so the exchange keeps its price near the coin's by making one side pay the other a small fee every hour or every eight hours. When leveraged longs crowd in, the perpetual trades at a premium and longs pay shorts. He, Manela, Ross and von Wachter treat that fee as the price of leverage demand,5 which ties it to the broader observation of Brunnermeier and Pedersen that what leverage costs depends on who is asking for it.6 Each exchange has its own crowd, so each has its own price of leverage. A difference paid over hours and days is a contest of patience, and speed buys very little in it.

Part two

The spread is real, and it lives where nobody trades

Over a year of hourly funding on 141 coins listed on both the first and second exchanges, the typical absolute spread is 12.5 percent a year. One coin-day in ten it is above 71 percent. On 21.5 percent of coin-days it is wide enough to pay for entering and leaving within a week.

That sounds like an open door. Two measurements say otherwise before any trade is simulated. The spread barely persists, since a sign lasts a median of two days, and the trailing day's spread explains only 16 percent of the next day's, with a slope of 0.41. Much of what a trader would see on the screen is already over by the time it is paid. And where the spread is wide, there is very little market to trade against.

The pattern has a simple mechanism. The first exchange sets funding from the premium of its own perpetual over the index, and the premium of a contract that few people trade wanders, because almost nothing anchors it. A thin market prints extreme funding for the same reason it prints wide spreads. Shleifer and Vishny argued that arbitrage capital is scarcest precisely where mispricing is largest,7 and Amihud showed that illiquidity is itself priced.8 Here the two arguments meet in a single scatter plot. At a position of 1 percent of a day's volume, a coin trading 50 thousand dollars a day takes a 500 dollar leg.

A wide funding spread on a thin contract is what a market looks like when almost no capital is there to close it.

Part three

Where the carry goes

The arm registered before any data was read collected funding at 88 percent a year on what it held. It kept 13 of those 88 points.

Signs that flip every two days force a portfolio ranked on them to turn over almost entirely every day, 0.96 of its notional on average. Each turn crosses the bid and ask four times, two legs on two venues, so costs took 63 percent a year. The gap between the two perpetuals' prices took another 12 percent. What remained, 13.2 percent at a t statistic of 1.48, came entirely from the first half of the year, with 26.1 percent before mid April and −0.7 percent after. The registered rule asks for a t above two and a positive second half, and the arm fails both.

Variants that post every order as a maker reach Sharpe ratios of eight on paper. They assume every resting order fills, which is the assumption Glosten and Milgrom warn against.9 A resting order fills when the market comes to it, usually because the price is about to move the other way. They are reported and never selected. The realistic cost used from the second round on assumes 60 percent of the notional fills as a maker and the rest crosses.

Part four

Every venue, every floor

The second round asked the question figure 2 forces. What is left once a coin has to trade a meaningful amount on both of its venues before the trade may touch it.

Each arena was rerun at three liquidity floors, with the variant chosen on the first half by Sharpe ratio and then measured on the second, untouched half. The t statistics use a Newey and West variance,10 and the best variant of each grid is deflated for the number of variants tried.11

The third exchange against the second is the most interesting failure, because it is robust. It earns about 1.2 percent a year at every floor, with a t of 2.6 at the highest, which is roughly 13 thousand dollars a year on a million dollars a leg. Two deep markets disagree a little, and consistently, and the disagreement is worth less than a savings account. A third round restricted every arena to coins trading over 200 million dollars a day. On the first exchange only bitcoin and ether qualify, and on the largest coins the spread between venues earns nothing in either pair, slightly less than nothing between the third and second.

Part five

The trade that survives is the oldest one

Every arena that earned anything shared one feature. It held the funding of a deep market rather than the difference between two of them.

Cash and carry is that trade in its plainest form. Buy the coin, sell the same amount short on its perpetual, and collect what longs pay shorts. Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen and Vrugt show that carry, defined as the return an asset earns if its price stays put, predicts returns across every major asset class.12 Schmeling, Schrimpf and Todorov document that crypto carry was enormous in 2020 and 2021 and has shrunk since, and that it rises with the demand for leverage from small traders.13

The fourth round ran it on bitcoin and ether at the first exchange's retail fees, over the second exchange's five-year history and over the first exchange's own year. Twelve rules were registered, from always holding to holding only while trailing funding exceeds a threshold, each with a seven and a thirty day window. The rule that always holds won on both histories.

The years tell the story the literature predicts. The carry earned 32.8 percent in 2021, 2.4 in 2022, 7.9 in 2023, 12.5 in 2024 and 5.0 in 2025, and is running near 2 percent a year in 2026. In 2024 to 2026 it earned 6.9 percent a year at a t statistic of 9.8, with its deepest drawdown under one percent over the whole sample. Over the first exchange's own year, on its own funding, perpetual and spot index, it earned 2.1 percent at a t of 2.9.

What the carry is paid for

Its exposure to price is close to nil, and its return still carries two risks. The return is the price of leverage, which can stay near zero for a year, as in 2022 and so far in 2026, and can turn negative for weeks. Its whole position sits on one exchange, so the failure of that exchange is a loss of the position rather than of a margin balance.

Nor is it a secret. It is the most widely known trade in crypto, and the compression since 2021 is what competition looks like when anyone can run it.

Part six

What the evidence will bear

The cross-venue result is a well-supported negative. Five arenas, three rounds and several thousand variants agree that the funding spread is wide only where it cannot be traded at a useful size. The one venue pair with depth on both sides, the third exchange against the second, pays about one percent a year.

The carry result is a positive with a regime attached. Its t statistics are large because its returns are smooth, which says little about how it will do next. Most of its five-year return came from two years, 2021 and 2024, when leverage was in heavy demand. The first exchange's own funding history covers one year, so its result rests mostly on the second exchange's history priced with the first's fees, and the spot leg uses the first exchange's index rather than fills on its spot book. Every variant tried across the four rounds counts against the best of them, and the grids were large.

Two things would change these conclusions. A second year of the first exchange's funding archive, collected daily since this study began, will let its result stand on its own data. And a period of strong leverage demand like 2021 or 2024 would show how much of the carry's history repeats when competition is as dense as it is now.

Methods

How the numbers were produced

Data

The first exchange's hourly funding, hourly candles and the spot index of its linear perpetuals, from October 2025. The second exchange's funding from its archive and its interface, its perpetual prices hourly and daily, and its spot prices, from 2021. The third exchange's funding and daily prices from its public interface, from 2021. A coin enters an arena on the days both of its legs have funding and prices.

The trade

A daily decision at midnight UTC uses only funding printed before it. The signal is the spread between the two venues' funding, annualised and averaged over a trailing window of eight hours to thirty days. A coin becomes active above an entry threshold and leaves below half of it, and the arm holds the most extreme active coins in equal weight. Funding is booked at each venue's own prints. The price leg is the long leg's return less the short leg's, from closes at midnight. Costs are each venue's taker fee, 3 basis points of half spread and 1 of slippage per side, or a blend with 60 percent at the maker fee. Spot pays 0.10 percent on the second exchange and 0.25 to 0.40 percent on the first, the latter at its retail tier.

Arenas and rounds

Round one ran 192 variants in each of five arenas, the first exchange against the second, the third against the second, the widest of the three, cash and carry on the second, and the second's spot against the first's perpetual. Round two added liquidity floors of 250 thousand, 1 million and 5 million dollars a day on the thinner leg, measured over the 30 days before each decision, and the blended cost. Round three restricted the arenas to coins over 200 million dollars a day. Round four ran twelve carry rules on bitcoin and ether at the first exchange's fees.

Statistics and protocol

Every round was written down before it ran, with its arms, its pass rule and a change log for anything clarified before results existed. Variants are selected on the first half of each history and measured on the second. t statistics use a Newey and West variance on daily returns,10 and the best variant of each grid is deflated over its grid's size.11 The margin each leg needs is measured from the largest single-leg move within a day among the positions actually held. Every figure is drawn by a script from the stored results, and the derived numbers in the text come from the same store.
